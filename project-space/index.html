The invitation

A block on campus called Technical Hub asked me to come.

The invitation came from Technical Hub, the wing at Aditya University that runs Project Space, and it was for two roles at once: give a talk to the participants, and sit on the jury panel that would judge their projects and declare the winners.

I said yes quickly, and I am glad I did. Travelling out to Surampalem for it meant a full day away from everything else, and every hour of it was worth it.

Season 7 ran under the banner “Frontiers in Technology and Beyond”, with the theme Project Life Cycle — the idea being that students should not just demo something that works, but carry an idea through the whole arc from problem to shipped product.

What it actually was

Not a showcase. A week-long, 24/7 innovation sprint.

Project Space was not another tech fest where teams turn up on the last day with a slide deck. Over 700+ participants lived inside the campus and built 120+ projects around the clock.

Picture it: entire corridors turned into code labs. Students asleep next to whiteboards. Debugging until dawn. Collaboration spilling across departments that would normally never share a room. All of it driven by one thing — the urge to create.

“They did not live, breathe, and build technology because a syllabus asked them to. They did it because they wanted to see the thing exist.”

The stack they reached for

What made it special was the sheer spread of technology.

Nobody handed these teams a template. They picked their own tools, and the range was genuinely surprising for an undergraduate cohort.

App & web

React, Flutter, Android and the web platform, used for genuinely modern app development rather than course projects.

GenAI & machine learning

Teams reaching for intelligent solutions, not as a buzzword but as the actual mechanism their idea needed to work.

VLSI & embedded

Hardware-level innovation from students who wanted to build closer to the metal than a web stack allows.

Blended builds

The most interesting projects refused to stay in one lane, blending software, hardware, design and purpose.

Projects that stayed with me

Eight I still remember by name.

Students as early as second year were pitching solutions for campus fixing, AI-enhanced education, donating for causes and more. These are a few that stuck.

How the day went

Speaker in the morning, judge by afternoon.

  1. The talk

    I spoke to the participants about building with conviction, thinking beyond theory, and what strong engineering standards and long-term growth actually look like once you are inside a real company.

  2. Walking the projects

    Then the better part — going stall to stall. I saw their projects, asked them questions, and interacted with hundreds of students who were far more prepared to defend their design decisions than I expected.

  3. The jury panel

    I sat on the jury alongside other invited speakers from across the industry. Comparing notes with them between rounds was its own kind of value.

  4. Choosing the winners

    We picked the winners and runners-up from 20 finalists narrowed out of 120+ projects. It was not an easy call. There was genuine brilliance across the board.

  5. Handing over the prizes

    I distributed the prizes on stage, and then they sent me home. A very good day by any measure.

Where these students went

Some of them are now interning at Google and Walmart.

This is the part I keep coming back to. A number of the students I met that week were selected into internship programmes at Google and Walmart, and several others have since been placed. The interaction I had with them and with the wider team was, in hindsight, a look at people who were already on their way.
